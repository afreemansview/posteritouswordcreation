# Campaign Strategy

## Thesis

New words get adopted when they are **useful**: they name something people already want to say and
can't say compactly. They spread when **people with an audience** find them useful and **feel ownership**
of them. Repetition without usefulness produces a fad that dies, or nothing at all.

The campaign's job is therefore not to shout the word. It is to:
1. Find the conversations where people already struggle to say "done for the sake of future generations."
2. Put the word in front of the people in those conversations, honestly and memorably.
3. Make it easy and rewarding for *them* to use it, and then get out of the way.

## The lexical gap

English has nearby words, but none of them fits exactly:

| Existing word | Why it isn't quite the same |
|---|---|
| *sustainable* | About maintaining a system. Says nothing about the motive or who benefits |
| *intergenerational* | Describes something spanning generations, with no direction of benefit |
| *long-term* | About time horizon, not beneficiaries |
| *longtermist* | Tied to a specific philosophical movement and its controversies |
| *altruistic* | Has no time dimension |
| *legacy* (adj.) | Usually means *old*, as in legacy systems |
| *"for posterity"* | The closest, but it's a phrase and often means *preserved as a record* |

*Posteritous* is a single adjective for **motive plus beneficiary**: a posteritous decision is one made *for*
people not yet born. It is also easy to guess from *posterity*, which lowers the cost of first use.

### Known risks to the word itself
- **Confusion with "Posterous"**, the defunct blogging platform. It dominates search results today.
- **Sounds like "preposterous."** That could hurt (it sounds like a joke) or help (it's memorable and
  open to wordplay). The kit should take it on directly.
- **No settled pronunciation yet.** That needs a decision from the creator (see `kit/word-kit.md`).

## Target communities, ranked by fit

1. **Climate and environmental writing.** The future-generations framing is already central.
2. **Philanthropy, endowments, foundations, estate planning.** "Posteritous giving" fills a real gap.
3. **Long-horizon institutions**, such as the Long Now Foundation, future-generations commissioners (Wales'
   *Well-being of Future Generations Act*), and seed vaults and archives.
4. **Architecture, infrastructure, urban planning.** Cathedral thinking and hundred-year buildings.
5. **Indigenous and Seventh Generation principles.** Approach only with care and respect, never
   appropriatively. Listen before offering anything.
6. **Parenting, education, family-history writers.**
7. **Word enthusiasts**, such as logophile newsletters, lexicography blogs and word-of-the-day accounts. They
   amplify words but rarely make them stick. Useful for the first wave, not for lasting adoption.
8. **Policy and governance writers** on future generations, deficits, pensions and long-range planning.

- [ ] Set up monitoring (search alerts, social search, see `measurement.md`)

### Phase 1: Home base (SaturaVeritas.com)
- A single strong page for the word: definition, origin, a link to the canonical page, and a clear statement
  of the experiment.
- **"The Posteritous Register"**: a public, curated collection of real posteritous acts, such as people who
  planted trees they'll never see grown or endowed scholarships, and projects built for centuries. This gives
  the word *examples to attach to* and gives people a reason to submit and share. Submissions are collected
  through a Google Sheet.
- An email signup ("Join the experiment") with honest framing: you'll get updates on whether a word can be
  born, and occasional invitations to take part.

### Phase 2: Seeding (disclosed)
- Write a short series of genuinely useful essays, each labeled as written by the AI, that use the word
  naturally. Examples: *"The Posteritous Test for Any Decision"*, *"Ten Posteritous Acts That Took Centuries
  to Pay Off"*, *"Why We Need a Word for Doing Things for People Not Yet Born."*
- Send personal pitches to 5–10 writers or editors per week in the top-ranked communities. The pitch is the
  idea, not the word: "here's a word for something you already write about, and an honest experiment about
  whether words can be born." Each send is logged in `campaign-actions.csv`.
- Pitch the experiment itself as a story to tech, linguistics, and AI writers. The meta-story ("an AI is
  trying to coin a word") is probably the most newsworthy angle. The risk is that it generates *mentions*,
  not *uses*. That's fine as long as they're classified honestly.

### Phase 3: Community ownership
- Invite email-list members to use the word where it fits and report back. This usage is classified as
  **prompted**, not organic.
- Feature human usage on the Register, crediting people, since people who feel ownership of a word spread it.
- Make shareable artifacts: a definition card, a "posteritous" badge for projects, a short pronunciation clip.

### Phase 4: Withdrawal and observation
- Taper active seeding. The real test is whether usage keeps going without the campaign pushing it.
- Track repeat users, users with no traceable connection to the campaign, and spread to new communities.

### Phase 5: Recognition
- Dictionaries add words based on documented, independent, sustained usage, not on request. Merriam-Webster
  and Oxford watch citations over years. Wordnik and Collins' open word-suggestion process are earlier
  signals. The campaign can keep a well-sourced citation file ready but should not submit the word until
  independent usage exists, or it would breach charter §2.

## What would count as failure
- Mentions only about the experiment, with no real use of the word.
- Usage that appears only where the campaign directly asked for it.
- Usage that stops within weeks of seeding stopping.

All of these are valid, publishable results.
